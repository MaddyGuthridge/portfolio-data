# I Am Two Halves of a Person

October 7th, 2026

I don't think it is controversial to say that people change over time. If I compare myself against myself from a decade ago, I consider myself to be a different person almost entirely. In fact, this is perhaps even truer for me than for most. I have a different name, a different appearance, different knowledge, and different values. This ability to change is something I consider central to living. Anyone who stops learning and stops growing also stops living. Change is a process of addition and subtraction. One adds on new parts, and drops old parts. We must curate our fractions such that our summation reflects our values and ideals for ourselves.

That change, however, is not always easy. The coupling between parts can lead us to take on aspects we don't like, or let go of parts we wish we could keep. I have been many things in the past, but most notably, I was Miguel. That is a part of me I don't wish to be anymore. It's not that I hate him. He is just a part of me that I have needed to say farewell to in order to embrace new parts. The Maddy part feels truer; more comfortable. But not necessarily happier. Until I say farewell to Miguel entirely though, I am still two halves of a person.

I get an email from my university. Student financials, specifically. They failed to verify my identity, and can no-longer prove that I am eligible for government financial support. I explain that I recently changed my legal name, and that it is taking some time to update my details with government services. I would appreciate if there is anything they can do to accommodate this time-consuming process. They are firm and direct. This is a legal requirement. They must verify my identity using my full legal name and date of birth. It doesn't matter if they verified it barely a month ago using my old name. If they cannot verify it by the university census date next week, they will unenrol me from all of my courses. I'll have to postpone my studies even further. It's that or pay thousands of dollars in course fees, which is even less doable. I cannot blame student financials. They are only a part of a system. They didn't decide to design an identity verification system based on names despite their mutability.

I call up the government. The MyID hotline, specifically. Australia's government is made up of many small parts, and they aren't especially interconnected -- a relic of our common law system, a friend tells me. Progress towards a single source of truth is slow, and not especially fruitful. The woman on the phone apologises. It looks like my change of name certificate cannot be used to update my details. Something about New South Wales not giving the federal government the ability to verify the document for MyID. The only solution is to completely delete and recreate my MyID entirely. It will take 10-14 calendar days, she says. "Is there anything I can do get this working faster?" I ask. She says that the only option is to manually update my details with each branch of government separately. It's a laborious task that fills me with dread. Of course, I cannot blame her. She is a small part of a large system. She wasn't the one who failed to consider the process through which people update their names when designing a new centralised identity system.

I log into the Australian Taxation Office website to update my details. Or more specifically, I attempt to. It won't let me. Something about my MyID identity strength decreasing since my last login, likely due to me unlinking it in preparation for its deletion. To increase the identity strength, I need to connect a new MyID to my MyGov account, and then reconnect that to the ATO website. My new MyID is over a week away, at least. I call them. Dial my way through the phone menus. Eventually, the system just gives up, tells me that the queue is full, and hangs up. The ATO is notoriously hard to contact by phone. It's an unfortunate coincidence. I updated my name right around tax time, so they are busier than ever. Needless to say, I cannot blame them for any of this. They are only a part of a larger system. It's not their fault that I need to update my details manually for every department in the government. Days later when I do contact them, they are nice enough. They assure me that my name is now updated. I cannot actually check. The act of logging into their website would reset my name back to the old name that MyGov still has, pending the recreation of my MyID. I try to resubmit my tax file number on UNSW's website. No changes. I email. No reply yet.

And so I find myself as two halves of a person. These systems were not designed for change; at least not for the kind of change I want. A marriage certificate would have been perfectly fine for their system. I wonder to myself whether it is fraudulent to marry-then-divorce someone for the purposes of an easier name change. Half of me is Miguel. It is a half that I no-longer see myself as. A part I wish to leave behind. Masculinity, manliness, and all of those fractions of me that were never comfortable. That half isn't even legally accurate anymore, but it's the half that the majority of the Australian government sees.

I wait for my old MyID to vanish. The pending destruction feels melancholic. I didn't want this change to be so... explosive? Wide-sweeping? It'll take a lot of things with it too. I won't be able to build up that same "identity strength" for some time. At least until my change of sex is approved and I get a new birth certificate and passport. I'm pretty sure that I won't be able to easily access the majority of the government's online services for weeks at least. The half of me that is Maddy doesn't have many ways to prove who she is yet. She wonders whether she even exists in a system that is not designed for her. I am two halves of a person, and neither of them are truly me. Half of me is Maddy. It is a half that I want to be. Maddy is enthusiastic, but gentle and empathetic. She has become a good listener, and a good explainer. But she also cannot verify her identity with the government. She isn't fully human. She exists only as a fraction.

Something I frequently tell my students is that software encodes a way of seeing the world. I think I heard that phrasing somewhere in a Philosophy Tube video or something. I wish I didn't have such concrete examples to show them. A student had practiced designing a Java class representing a university employee. They forgot the `setName` method. I laugh. It's easy to see how they would have overlooked it. Most of my students were very confused when a site I logged into greeted me with a "Good afternoon, Miguel". Despite the fact that I am visibly and audibly transgender, I guess they must have assumed that I had always been Maddy for some reason. It's so easy to assume immutability. As a software engineer, I often consider immutability to be a virtue in design. So many bugs are avoided. The ease of parallelism makes code faster, and the consistency grants a beautiful simplicity. But the world is not simple. People are messy. People change. And our systems need to account for that.

The problem is that accounting for it is incredibly difficult. There is no individual failure in any particular system that can be blamed entirely. It's not UNSW's fault that the government forces them to use an arcane identity verification system which assumes immutability. It's not the ATO's fault that their site expects people to create their digital ID once and then keep it forever. It's not MyID's fault that NSW (allegedly) doesn't support them verifying change of name certificates. It's not the NSW government's fault that I need my change of name to be completed so swiftly under threat of being forced to half my education. No one organisation or person decided that "hmmm today I will make the process of updating one's legal name across government systems and educational institutions especially painful and tedious for trans people". Fixing any one of these oversights would have made this so much less stressful. And yet I can't blame any of them; they are all just small parts of the problem. But the system is still composed of those parts. No one part is transphobic, but they still contribute to a system which discriminates against trans people.

And so, what can we as humans do about this? How can we make sure that people aren't adversely impacted by the systems we build? I believe that unless a disproportionate time is spent considering the needs of marginalised people, the world we create will disproportionately discriminate against said marginalised people. We have a moral responsibility not to build systems that encode a way of seeing the world where some people cannot exist.

I am two halves of a person, but I still add up to a whole. I deserve to be seen as myself. I deserve to be able to change without the systems around me forcing me out of my education. I deserve to be able to express the ways in which I have changed without spending hours fighting to be seen (hours which I probably should have spent working so I can afford to survive the summer break).

Even still, despite the tedium, I continue to persist. I don't think it is controversial to say that people change over time. As such, I shall keep learning and growing. To learn and grow is to live.

Until next time,<br>
Maddy (mostly *she*, but a bit of *they*, and a bit of *it* if you're a close friend)
